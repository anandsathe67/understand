A short book about the Riemann hypothesis

How Wobbly Are the Primes?

In 2000, a foundation in America put up a million dollars for one problem about prime numbers. Many brilliant people have tried. The prize is still unclaimed. In November 2026 a proof was claimed, and experts are checking it now.

The problem is called the Riemann hypothesis. Most explanations show a strange graph and stop. Others promise that solving it would break the internet. It wouldn't. This book builds the idea from scratch, one step at a time, with things you can poke. You'll need counting, powers, square roots, a coin and a graph. Nothing more.

    The two-minute version

    The Riemann hypothesis in two minutes

    A prime is a number you cannot split into equal rows: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, and on forever.

    Primes thin out as numbers grow. A smooth curve predicts how many there are up to any point. The prediction is very good, but never exact. It wobbles.

    What it says

    The Riemann hypothesis says the primes are spread about as evenly as they could be. Their wobble stays small, like the drift in a long run of coin flips. In numbers, the error stays inside a band about the size of the square root. (The exact statement adds a slowly growing extra factor.)

    Where it comes from

    In 1859, Bernhard Riemann linked the primes to one function, now called the zeta function. Each place where it equals zero adds one ripple to the prime count. The hypothesis says every one of those zeros lies on one straight line. If even one zero strayed off it, the primes would wobble more than coin flips.

    Computers have checked more than 12 trillion zeros. Every one sits on the line. But checking is not proving. Until November 2026, nobody had a proof.

    What would change

    If it is provedMany theorems that begin "if the Riemann hypothesis is true" become facts. Some fast computer methods for primes gain proven guarantees, though they already work in practice. The new ideas in the proof may matter even more.
    If it is disprovedThose theorems lose their proofs. Some may still be true, but would need new ones.
    Your phone and your bankNothing changes either way. Encryption depends on how hard it is to split a huge number into its prime factors. The hypothesis says nothing about that.

    Since 2000, a proof has carried a prize of one million dollars. It is still unclaimed.

    News, November 2026: two proofs have been claimed. One was built with an AI and checked step by step by computer. Experts are still checking both. If it holds, the main uses are guarantees for computer methods that already work. The bigger change may be how it was made.
    Take this with youThe Riemann hypothesis says the primes wobble no more than chance would. It matters to mathematics far more than to technology.

    Chapter 1

    Primes are the numbers you cannot split into equal rows

    Let's start with buttons.

    Take 12 buttons. You can lay them out in 2 rows of 6. Or 3 rows of 4. Twelve is easy to tidy into a rectangle.

    Now take 7 buttons. Try 2 rows: one is left over. Try 3 rows: one is left over again. Every try leaves a straggler. The only tidy layout is one long row.

    A number that only fits in one long row is called a prime. The first few are . One dot is a special case. It doesn't count as prime.

    Chapter 2

    Primes thin out, but in a pattern

    They thin out in a pattern. Let's find it.

    A kitchen sieve lets the fine stuff fall through. A number sieve does the same with multiples. Cross out every multiple of 2, except 2. Then every multiple of 3. Then 5, then 7. Whatever survives is prime.

    Now count further. A quick word on powers first. 10 × 10 × 10 is "10 to the power 3", written 10³. It is a 1 followed by three zeros.

    Each extra digit makes primes rarer by almost the same step. The steps settle near . So near a million, about one number in is prime. The table averages over all the smaller numbers too, so its figures are a bit lower.

    Around 1792, a teenage Carl Friedrich Gauss spotted this pattern in tables of primes. He turned it into a smooth guess. Measure a number's digits in steps of instead of 1. That measure is the natural logarithm. Near a number, the chance of being prime is one over its natural logarithm. Add up those chances, number by number, and you get Gauss's smooth guess for the prime count.

    A few things to notice

    • The count keeps growing. Primes never run out.
    • The step is never exactly the same. It creeps toward one size.
    Take this with youPrimes thin out steadily: each extra digit adds about to "one in every". Gauss turned this into a smooth guess.

    How close does the smooth guess come to the true count?

    History: Gauss's tables

    Gauss counted primes for fun, a block of a thousand numbers at a time. Over his life his tables reached about three million. He had no computer. The sieve on this page does a million in a blink.

    History: primes never run out

    Euclid proved this about 300 BC. Multiply any list of primes and add 1. The result leaves remainder 1 when divided by each prime on the list. So its own prime factors are missing from the list. For 2, 3, 5 and 7, you get , a new prime.

    Side note: why the sieve can stop at 7

    Any number up to 100 that is not prime has a factor no bigger than its square root, which is at most . The primes up to that are 2, 3, 5 and 7. So after 7, nothing new gets crossed out. Try pressing once more.

    Chapter 3

    The guess is good, but it wobbles

    It comes very close, but it is never exact.

    Picture a staircase next to a smooth ramp. The true prime count is the staircase. It steps up by one at each prime. Gauss's guess is the ramp. Zoom out and see how well the ramp hugs the stairs.

    As a share, the guess gets better and better. Up to a million it is off by only %. But the gap itself, counted in primes, wobbles up and down. And it slowly grows.

    Here is a warning for the whole book. Checking is not proving. Up to a million the guess is always too high. Computers have checked far beyond a million, and it is still too high. Yet in 1914 John Littlewood proved it must dip too low, again and again, somewhere far out. Patterns in primes can hold for a very long time and then break. So in this book we check things to see them, never to settle them.

    A few things to notice

    • The gap is tiny next to the count.
    • The gap is not steady. It rises and falls as you zoom.
    Take this with youThe smooth guess is excellent as a share. What matters next is the size of its errors, the wobbles.

    How big should a wobble be? What would count as "small"?

    History: the prime number theorem

    In 1896, Jacques Hadamard and Charles de la Vallée Poussin proved that the share error shrinks toward zero forever. This is the prime number theorem. Both proofs used Riemann's ideas from chapters 7 and 8.

    Chapter 4

    Pure chance wobbles by a square root

    A good yardstick for a wobble is pure chance.

    Flip a coin. Score one point for heads and lose one for tails. Keep a running total. It's like a walker taking random steps left and right. Where does the walker end up?

    First, square roots. The square root of 100 is , because 10 × 10 = 100. The square root of 10,000 is .

    You might fear a drift of thousands after 10,000 flips. Instead it usually ends within about . Heads and tails mostly cancel. What's left over grows like the square root of the number of flips.

    Notice the band's edges are curved. Square roots grow fast at first, then ever more slowly. So ten times more flips gives only about times more drift. A hundred times more flips gives times more.

    Hold on to this ideaPure chance drifts by about the square root. After N flips, expect a drift near √N.

    A few things to notice

    • Some runs poke outside the band. The band is a typical size, not a wall.
    • Run the crowd: about two people in three end inside the band.
    Take this with youRandom wobbles grow, but slowly: like the square root of the number of tries.

    Do the primes wobble around Gauss's guess like coin flips, or more?

    For the curious: why a square root?

    Each flip adds +1 or −1. Square the total and average over many runs. The cross terms cancel, because heads and tails are equally likely. What survives is one for each flip. So the average squared drift is N, and the typical drift is √N. The crowd's "typical drift" in the readout is exactly this: the square root of the average squared total.

    Chapter 5

    The hypothesis: primes wobble no more than coin flips

    As far as anyone can tell, they wobble no more.

    Think of a car in a lane. The lane widens as the road goes on. The car may swerve, but never leaves the lane. Here the car is the gap between Gauss's guess and the true count. The lane is the square-root band from the coin flips.

    Here is the Riemann hypothesis, in plain words. The gap between Gauss's guess and the prime count never grows faster than coin-flip drift. Up to a number x, the gap stays within about √x.

    An honest note. The exact statement uses a slightly different band. It is √x times the natural logarithm of x, divided by about . At a million that band is , even narrower. Far out it grows a little faster than √x, but only slowly. Tick the box to see it.

    A few things to notice

    • Up to a million, the gap never leaves the plain band. Its tightest moment uses % of it, at .
    • Far out, the gap uses only a small part of the band.
    Take this with youThe primes wobble around Gauss's guess no more than coin flips wobble around zero. Give or take a slow factor, that is the hypothesis.

    Riemann never mentioned coins. Where did his idea come from?

    Side note: the real coin flips inside the primes

    The coin picture is a loose one. Here is a closer match. Count the prime factors of a whole number, with repeats: 12 = 2 × 2 × 3 has three. Score +1 if the count is even, and −1 if it is odd. Keep a running total, like heads minus tails. The hypothesis is true exactly when this total drifts no faster than coin flips, give or take a tiny extra power. And if the hypothesis holds, the gap from Gauss's guess is usually even smaller than the square root.

    History: the band's exact size

    In 1901 Helge von Koch showed this band statement is equivalent to Riemann's hypothesis. In 1976 Lowell Schoenfeld made it exact: if the hypothesis is true, the gap is below √x times the natural logarithm of x, divided by 8π, for every x from 2,657 on. The dashed lines show that exact band.

    Chapter 6

    A sum over all numbers equals a product over primes

    It came from Leonhard Euler, who tied all numbers to the primes in 1737.

    Powers first. 4 is 2 squared, 2 × 2, written 2². And 9 is 3². Now add one over every square: 1 + 1/4 + 1/9 + 1/16 and so on. The total creeps up but never runs away. Watch where it lands.

    Now use only primes. For the prime 2, take 1 + 1/4 + 1/16 + … Each piece is a quarter of the one before. Call the total T. Then T is 1 plus a quarter of T. So T is 4/3. Do the same for 3, for 5, for every prime. Multiply the results together.

    Multiplying out the brackets gives one over every square, each exactly once. That's because each number splits into primes in only one way. So the prime product and the full sum agree.

    The total, as the dial turns, is called the zeta function, after the Greek letter ζ.

    Not every endless sum settles. Take 1 + ½ + ¼ + ⅛ and so on. Each piece fills half the gap that is left, so it settles at 2. Now take 1 + ½ + ⅓ + ¼ and so on. Its pieces shrink too, but it grows forever. That is the dial at 1, the runaway point.

    A few things to notice

    • The product uses far fewer pieces, yet keeps up.
    • Turn the dial down to 1. After a thousand numbers the sum is , and it keeps growing.
    Take this with youZeta adds one over every number raised to the dial's power. The same total is a product over primes alone. So zeta "knows" the primes.

    So far the dial took ordinary numbers. What if it turns somewhere stranger?

    History: the Basel problem

    The sum at dial 2 puzzled mathematicians for decades. Euler solved it in 1735: it equals π²/6, which is (rounded). Two years later he found the prime product.

    Chapter 7

    The dial can turn sideways

    Stranger settings exist: the dial can point sideways.

    Face east. Multiply by −1 and you face west: a half turn. What, done twice, makes a half turn? A quarter turn. Call it i. Then i × i = −1. Here, i just means "turn a quarter".

    A number like 3 + 2i means 3 steps east, then 2 steps north. It's a point on a map. Put such a point into the dial. Each piece of the sum becomes an arrow with a size and a turn. Take the turning rule on trust. Then add the arrows, tip to tail.

    At 2 + 3i the arrows shrink fast. The spiral winds in to one point, zeta's value. Now try ½ + 14.13i. The arrows shrink too slowly. The tip circles on and never settles. West of the line through 1, the plain sum stops working.

    A step we take on trust. Picture two functions at once. The first is the plain sum. The second agrees with it wherever the sum works. It also makes sense everywhere else on the map, except at 1. Riemann built it in 1859. Only one smooth function can do this. That one is the full zeta. The proof needs tools beyond this book.

    A few things to notice

    • Where all the colours meet at a dark point, zeta is zero.
    • Those dark points line up, where the east part is one half.
    • The white point at 1 is the runaway sum.
    Take this with youWith a sideways dial, zeta becomes a coloured map. Its zeros seem to line up on the line where the east part is ½.

    Are those zeros really on the line? Can we find them ourselves?

    For the mathematically curious: the stretch

    The trick is analytic continuation. Alternate the signs: 1 − 1/2s + 1/3s − … This works for every east part above 0. Dividing by 1 − 21−s gives zeta there. Riemann then proved a mirror rule linking s and 1 − s, which covers the rest. This page computes zeta with Euler–Maclaurin summation: add the first terms, then correct for the tail very accurately. Read more about i as a turn in this book on Euler's identity.

    Side note: the easy zeros

    The stretched zeta is also zero at −2, −4, −6 and so on. These come straight from the mirror rule. They are called the trivial zeros. The hypothesis is about the others, inside the strip between east parts 0 and 1.

    Chapter 8

    The zeros sit on the line

    Yes, and you can find them. Walk up the line.

    Imagine hiking a coastal path with an altimeter. One moment you are above sea level. The next you are below. You must have crossed sea level in between.

    Walk up the line where the east part is ½. Zeta's arrow there carries a known twist. It comes with the same proven stretch from chapter 7, so take it on trust too. Undo the twist and the arrow points straight east or west. So it becomes a plain number, plus or minus. When it changes sign, zeta has passed through zero.

    Watch the arrow panel too. The tip of zeta's arrow draws a swirl as you climb. Each time the swirl passes through the centre, the arrow has length zero. That is a zero.

    Riemann guessed in 1859 that every zero between east parts 0 and 1 lies on this line. That is the Riemann hypothesis, in its usual form. Computers have checked about trillion zeros. All are on the line.

    A few things to notice

    • The zeros are not evenly spaced. They crowd closer as you climb.
    • The leftover sideways part stays at zero. The twist really works.
    Take this with youThe Riemann hypothesis: every zero of zeta in the strip sits on the line where the east part is ½.

    These are zeros of a function of made-up numbers. What do they have to do with primes?

    History: one paper, a famous list, and tea

    Riemann's paper of 1859 was eight pages long. It was the only one he ever wrote about primes. Riemann had been Gauss's doctoral student at Göttingen, finishing in 1851. His ideas about curved space later became the maths of Einstein's general relativity. In 1900 David Hilbert put the hypothesis on his famous list of problems for the new century. In 1914 G. H. Hardy proved that infinitely many zeros lie on the line. In 1972 Hugh Montgomery met Freeman Dyson over tea in Princeton. Montgomery's formula for the spacing of zeros matched Dyson's formula for energy levels in heavy atomic nuclei. Nobody fully knows why. In 2021 Dave Platt and Tim Trudgian confirmed every zero up to height 3 × 10¹² is on the line.

    Chapter 9

    Each zero is a wave in the prime count

    They shape the primes. Every zero adds one ripple, and enough ripples rebuild the staircase of primes.

    Think of a choir. One pure note is a smooth wave. Add many notes and you can make almost any shape of sound. Even a sharp click.

    A step we take on trust. Riemann wrote down an exact formula linking zeros to primes. Hans von Mangoldt proved it in full in 1895. It takes the smooth curve and adds one wave for each zero. We can't derive it here. But we can check it.

    With no waves, the curve is smooth. It misses every step. The first wave already bends it toward the stairs. Each new zero adds a faster ripple. Press Hunt to reach height 250 for about a hundred waves. Then the curve leaps at and every prime after. You can read the primes off a sum of waves.

    The smooth curve here is Gauss's guess with a small correction from Riemann. It allows for squares, cubes and higher powers of primes.

    The switch shows a simpler version, the weighted count. Each prime adds its natural logarithm instead of 1. At 7 it steps up by about . Powers like 4, 8 and 9 add a step too. With these weights, the smooth part is a plain straight line. That is why experts prefer it.

    A few things to notice

    • Each wave is small near 2 and slowly grows.
    • More waves make the jumps sharper. The jumps land on primes.
    • Between primes, the waves cancel into a flat step.
    Take this with youThe prime staircase is a smooth curve plus one wave per zero. Zeros are the notes. Primes are the tune.

    How tall does each wave grow? And why does one half matter?

    For the mathematically curious: the formula

    Write ρ for a zero, ½ + iγ. Riemann's R(x) is Gauss's curve with corrections for prime powers. The page draws

    π(x) ≈ R(x) − Σρ R(xρ) − 1/ln x + arctan(π/ln x)/π

    Each zero and its mirror below give one real wave. Its size near x is about 2√x / (|ρ| ln x). The page computes R(xρ) with the complex exponential integral, using every term with x1/n ≥ 2. The last two terms come from the trivial zeros.

    The weighted count is ψ(x), the sum of ln p over every prime power pk up to x. Its formula has no logarithmic integral at all:

    ψ(x) = x − Σρ xρ/ρ − ln 2π − ½ ln(1 − x−2)

    Each zero and its mirror give the wave 2 Re(xρ/ρ). At a jump, both sides average.

    Chapter 10

    One half on the line means a square-root wobble

    A zero's east part sets how fast its wave grows. One half gives a square root.

    Think of interest on savings. A rate of 5% or 6% seems close. Over a lifetime, the gap is huge. Powers behave the same way. A small change in the power becomes a giant change in size.

    A wave from a zero with east part ½ swings by about x to the power ½. And x to the power ½ is just √x. For a million: . That is the coin-flip size you held on to. Now move a zero to east part 0.75. A million to the power 0.75 is . At a trillion the two are and .

    On the line, the wave swings less than √x, forever. Off the line, it always breaks out eventually. Zeros come in mirror pairs, so the twin on the other side can't cancel it. One zero off the line would make the primes wobble more than coin flips.

    A few things to notice

    • A tiny move still breaks out. It just takes longer.
    • The mirror twin's wave shrinks against the band.
    Take this with you"All zeros on the line" and "primes wobble like coin flips" are the same claim. One half is the square root.

    So what would change if someone proved it, or found a zero off the line?

    Side note: many waves at once

    This shows a single wave. The real gap adds up all the waves. That is why the exact band has the small extra factor from chapter 5. With every zero on the line, the total stays inside it.

    Chapter 11

    Proving it would not break your phone's encryption

    In your daily life, nothing would change.

    Your phone, your bank and the internet would work as before. So would physics. What changes is what mathematicians know for certain.

    Phone encryption rests on a different problem. Multiply two huge primes: easy. Split the answer back into them: nobody knows a fast way. The hypothesis is about how primes are spread on average. It says nothing about splitting one number. Knowing how many people live in each town won't tell you anyone's phone number.

    Hundreds of theorems begin "if the Riemann hypothesis is true". A proof would turn them into facts. A disproof would leave those proofs unfinished. Many of the results could still be true, but they would need new proofs. A zero off the line would sit higher than 3 × 10¹², the height already checked.

    A few things to notice

    • Encryption never changes, whatever you pick.
    • Two results were once proved only by assuming a stronger version of the hypothesis. Both now have proofs without it.

    Either way, the new methods would probably matter more than the answer. That's why mathematicians care.

    Take this with youA proof turns many "ifs" into facts. A disproof sends some back to work. Your phone doesn't notice either way.

    News: a proof was claimed in November 2026

    On 20 November 2026, Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, said he had found a proof. He worked with Claude, an AI model. He posted a four-page paper. A much longer version is written in Lean, a computer language in which a machine checks every step.

    The Lean version is huge. Reports describe about 175,000 files. Independent checkers say the files compile, and the Lean community has said it is confident the proof is correct. Reports say it also covers a wider version, the generalised hypothesis.

    But no person has yet followed the whole argument. Checkers are still confirming that the statement proved is exactly the right one. Until mathematicians accept it, this book treats the hypothesis as unproved. If it holds, the "Proved" column above is the one that comes true.

    The same day, Ravi Vakil, president of the American Mathematical Society, posted a second claim. It is a 940-page manuscript by Alessandro Masullo, reported to be a postdoctoral researcher at Stanford. It claims a proof of the generalised hypothesis. It is being checked too.

    If it holds, what would it be used for? Mostly guarantees. Some fast computer methods for primes were only known to work if a stronger version is true. The claimed proof reportedly covers that version. So these methods would become proven. One is a quick test for whether a number is prime. Searching for a large prime, as making a key does, would also get a proven time limit.

    In practice little changes, because these methods already work. Some famous questions need even wider versions, so they would stay open. Encryption would not change.

    The bigger effect may be the method. It reportedly extends an older counting technique of Sergei Stepanov, which could open related problems. And a machine-checked proof this large shows what AI and proof checkers can do. The same kind of checker is used on software, chips and security protocols.

    History: a famous claim

    In 2018 Sir Michael Atiyah, a celebrated mathematician, presented a short claimed proof. Experts quickly found it did not work, and it was not accepted. Many claimed proofs appear every year. Before 2026, none survived checking.

    Why care, if nothing changes?

    Primes are the atoms of whole numbers. The hypothesis says their spread is as tame as it could possibly be. Nearly every deep tool in number theory touches it. A proof would likely bring new tools, the way the 1896 proof of the prime number theorem did.

    Make your own test

    Here is a test anyone can run on whole numbers. Think of it as a speed limit for one number's divisors.

    Add up all the divisors of a number. For 12: 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 6 + 12 = . Now build a limit from fractions. Let H be 1 + 1/2 + 1/3, all the way to 1/12. The limit is H plus eH times the natural log of H. Here e is a fixed number, about , and eH undoes the natural log. For 12 the limit is .

    In 2002 Jeffrey Lagarias showed something startling. The hypothesis is true exactly when no number ever breaks this limit.

    A few things to notice

    • The closest any small number comes is , at % of its limit. Try and too.
    • Even a huge pass is a check, not a proof.

    No amount of checking can prove the hypothesis this way. But a single number that broke the limit would disprove it at once. That is the strange power of a counterexample.

    Take this with youPrimes wobble around Gauss's guess. Riemann's zeros set the size of the wobble. If every zero sits on the line, the wobble is coin-flip sized.
    Try it at home
    • Rectangles of coins. Take 2 to 30 coins or buttons and try to make rectangles. Numbers that only make one long row are prime. It works because a rectangle is a multiplication. Same idea as the dots sim in chapter 1.
    • Flip a real coin 100 times. Score heads minus tails. You'll most likely end within about 10 of zero, the square root of 100. Same as the coin sim in chapter 4.
    • Sieve on squared paper. Write 1 to 100 in a 10 by 10 grid. Cross out multiples of 2, 3, 5 and 7. It works because every non-prime up to 100 has a factor of 10 or less. Same as the sieve sim in chapter 2.

    In one sentence: primes wobble like coin flips exactly when every zero sits on Riemann's line. Nobody yet knows if they all do.
