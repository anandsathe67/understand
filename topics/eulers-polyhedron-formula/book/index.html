A short interactive book

Why Always Two?

How every solid shape hides the same number, and how you can prove it yourself.

Picture a football lying on wet grass. Black patches, white patches. Count the black ones. There are 12. Pick up any proper football in the world and count again: 12. Not 11. Not 13. Why?

This book is for you to work through on your own. Every picture is a real shape the computer is holding. You can turn it, poke it, cut it up and count it. Nothing is drawn by hand, so nothing can fib.

    Chapter 1

    Every solid shape has corners, edges and faces

    The football's secret starts somewhere much smaller: with counting.

    Think of a camping tent. The poles meet at sharp points. The poles themselves run in straight lines. Between the poles hang flat panels of cloth. Every solid shape is built the same way. The sharp points are called corners. The straight lines are edges. The flat panels are faces.

    Turn the shape below with your finger or mouse. Pick what to count, then tap the shape to mark each one. The sentence underneath keeps the tally.

    A few things to notice

    • Choose the cube and mark its corners. You can't see them all at once. Turn it, or press “See through”.
    • Try the dodecahedron and count its faces. Then count the corners of the icosahedron. Spot anything?
    • Pick an edge on any shape. Look at the two faces on either side of it.

    Hold on to this idea

    Every edge has exactly two faces touching it, one on each side. Never one, never three. This small fact pays off twice later in the book.

    Take this with you

    Corners are points, edges are lines, faces are flat panels. Every solid shape has three numbers: how many of each.

    Try it at home: toothpicks and plasticine

    Roll twelve pea-sized balls of plasticine. Push toothpicks into them to build a cube: 8 balls, 12 sticks. Balls are corners, sticks are edges, and the empty windows are faces. Plasticine grips the sticks. Soft dough slumps and lets go, so don't use that. Count your model next to the cube in the sim. Same three numbers.

    So now every shape gives you three numbers. Is there a pattern hiding in them?

    Chapter 2

    Count enough shapes and one number keeps turning up

    Yes, there is a pattern. But this book won't tell you what it is. You are going to find it.

    A detective fills a notebook with facts, one line at a time, until something clicks. This table is your notebook. The shapes you looked at in Chapter 1 are already written in. Add more with the menu. Then stare at the three columns. Try adding two of them. Try taking one away from another.

    shapecornersedgesfacescorners + facesedges + 2

    A few things to notice

    • Add the pyramids. Their corner and face counts match. Do the edges follow?
    • Add every prism. Each one has the same gap between two of its columns.
    • Only press “Stuck?” after a real try. The hint spoils the hunt.
    Did you find it? Open to compare

    Corners plus faces is always two more than edges. Said the other way round: corners − edges + faces = 2. Check it on the cube: 8 − 12 + 6 = 2. On the football: 60 − 90 + 32 = 2. Every row in your table agrees.

    Take this with you

    For every shape in the table, corners − edges + faces comes to 2. That number now sits in the top bar and follows you through the book.

    Side note: who noticed first?

    René Descartes scribbled something close to this in private notes about 1630 and never printed them. Leonhard Euler wrote the rule in a letter to his friend Goldbach in 1750, and admitted he had no proof. Dates from memory; see the footer.

    Eleven shapes agree. But is it always 2, or did we just get lucky? And why 2?

    Chapter 3

    Squash any shape flat and nothing changes

    To find out why, we stop collecting shapes and start changing one.

    Peel an orange carefully. Cut one hole, and you can spread the whole peel flat on the table. Pretend our shape is made of stretchy rubber. Pop one face open, like a hole in a balloon. Pull the hole wide and press everything else flat. You get a map: dots, lines and patches.

    Pick a shape. Tap a face to pop it, then drag the slider to flatten.

    Here is the part that keeps the count honest. The popped face has not vanished. It has become the whole outside of the map, and its fence of edges is still there. So the outside counts as one face. Then nothing has changed: same corners, same edges, same number of faces.

    A few things to notice

    • Flatten the cube. Count the patches inside the map: 5. Add the outside: 6. Same as the cube.
    • Pop a different face on the same shape. The map looks different, but the three numbers don't move.
    • Try the football. Even 90 edges press flat without crossing.

    Take this with you

    Any of our shapes can be popped and pressed into a flat map. The outside is a face. The three numbers survive the squash.

    Try it at home: a cube of straws

    Cut 12 straws to one length. Join them with pipe cleaners bent inside the straw ends, three at each corner. Now push the top square wide open and press the cube flat onto the table. That is the sim's slider, done with your hands. Pipe cleaners bend; glue would snap.

    Side note: the flattening trick has a birthday

    Augustin Cauchy published a proof built on this squash in 1813, about sixty years after Euler's letter. Date from memory; see the footer.

    Now we have a flat map instead of a solid. Why is that better? Because a flat map can be snipped.

    Chapter 4

    Snip the map down to a dot and the number never moves

    A flat map is better because we can take it apart one snip at a time. After every snip, we check the number.

    It is like pulling bricks off a wall and weighing it after each brick. Only two snips are allowed:

    Cut a line that has a different patch on each side. Remember the idea you held on to: every edge has two faces beside it. Cut it and those two patches merge into one. So edges go down by 1 and faces go down by 1.

    Snip off a loose corner, a dot with just one line. The dot goes and its line goes. Corners down 1, edges down 1.

    Tap a line to cut it, or a loose dot to snip it off. Your map from Chapter 3 carries on here.

    Watch the arithmetic box. Edges are a take-away. Taking away one fewer edge pushes the total up by 1. Losing a face pulls it down by 1. Up one, down one: it stays.

    A few things to notice

    • Cut lines until every patch has merged into the outside. Now only “snip a loose corner” works.
    • Try cutting a line with the same patch on both sides. The map refuses, because that would split it in two.
    • Keep going to a single dot: 1 − 0 + 1 = 2. One corner, no lines, one patch: the outside.

    Take this with you

    Each snip removes one thing that adds and one thing that takes away. The number can't move. The last dot gives 2, so the map gave 2, so the shape gave 2.

    Try it at home: the pencil snip game

    Draw any map on paper: dots joined by lines, none crossing. Write its three numbers. Rub out a line between two patches, or a loose dot with its line. Write the numbers again. Tally corners − edges + faces at each step. It never leaves 2.

    Snipping never moved the number. What about growing? Could adding things push it off 2?

    Chapter 5

    Growing the map doesn't change it either

    No. Adding things can't push it off 2, and you can watch why.

    Think of a town adding streets. A new road between two corners of a field splits that field into two. A new dead-end lane adds one corner at its end and one line to reach it. Those are the only ways to grow a map, and each one adds a pair.

    Your map carries on from the last chapter, even if it is down to a dot. Pick a move and tap.

    Join two corners across a patch: edges up 1, faces up 1. Sprout a corner inside a patch: corners up 1, edges up 1. Split a line with a new dot: corners up 1, edges up 1. Every move adds one thing that counts for and one that counts against.

    A few things to notice

    • Grow a dot into a big map with twenty corners. The total still reads 2.
    • Try joining two corners that already share a line. The map says no: that would be the same line twice.
    • Sprout a corner, then try to join it across a patch so the new line would cross another. Refused, because lines on a map never cross.

    Take this with you

    Growing and snipping are the same moves run backwards. Neither can budge the number. Any map you can draw gives 2.

    Side note: this works for maps of countries too

    Look at your map as countries. Patches are countries, lines are borders, dots are the points where borders meet. The outside is the sea. Border points − borders + countries = 2. Draw any atlas page this way and it holds, sea included.

    So snipping and growing both leave 2 alone. Does that settle it for every shape? That depends on what “settle” means.

    Chapter 6

    That was a proof, not a lucky streak

    To settle something you need a reason that covers every case, not a pile of examples.

    There are two ways to be sure the sun rises tomorrow. One is to have seen it rise every day so far. The other is to understand how the Earth turns. The first is checking. The second is proving. Chapter 2 was checking. Chapters 3 to 5 were a proof, and here it is in one breath:

    Take any of our shapes. Pop a face and press it flat; the numbers survive. Snip it down; every snip keeps the number. You end at a dot, and a dot gives 2. So the shape gave 2. No step needed trust. You did each one yourself.

    The shrinking machine: it pops, flattens and snips any shape you choose, checking the number at every step.

    A few things to notice

    • Run the machine on the football. Ninety edges, and the number never twitches once.
    • Press “One step” and predict the next line of arithmetic before it appears.
    • Ask: did any step depend on which shape it was? None did. That is what makes it a proof.

    Take this with you

    Checking shows a rule has held so far. Proving shows it must hold, even for shapes nobody has built. You now own a proof.

    Side note: a cousin puzzle from 1736

    The town of Königsberg had seven bridges. People asked whether a walk could cross each bridge exactly once. Euler showed it couldn't, by reasoning rather than trying every route. Try it below: tap bridges in order.

    Date from memory; see the footer.

    You own the rule now. A good place to stop for the night. Tomorrow, make it work for you: back to the football.

    Chapter 7

    Every football needs exactly 12 pentagons

    The rule can tell us, before we count, that a football must have 12 pentagons.

    Three facts to check on the ball below. Every patch is a pentagon or a hexagon. Exactly three edges meet at every corner. And every edge has two patches beside it, the idea you held on to.

    A real football shape, the truncated icosahedron. Tap a patch to count its corners; tap a corner to count its edges.

    Now share the ball out between its patches. A corner is shared by 3 patches, so chop it into 3 slivers. An edge is shared by 2, so chop it into 2 halves.

    Six pentagons hold 30 slivers, which is 10 corners, and 30 halves, which is 15 edges. 10 − 15 + 6 = 1. Six hexagons hold 36 slivers, 12 corners, and 36 halves, 18 edges. 12 − 18 + 6 = 0.

    So hexagons add nothing, however many there are. Only pentagons move the total, 1 for every six. The total must be 2. Two sixes. Twelve pentagons.

    A few things to notice

    • Highlight the pentagons and turn the ball. No two pentagons touch. Count them: 12.
    • The arithmetic box adds up slivers and halves from the real ball. Check its answers against the counts.
    • Thirteen pentagons would push the total past 2.

    Take this with you

    Sharing corners and edges out among patches turns the rule into a tool. It predicted 12 before you counted.

    Try it at home: a real ball and 12 stickers

    Find a classic football. Stick a sticker on every black pentagon. Count the stickers: 12. Now pick any corner: exactly one pentagon touches it. So 12 × 5 = 60 corners, without counting them.

    Side note: a football made of atoms

    Sixty carbon atoms can join up in exactly this shape: the buckyball. Its discoverers won the 1996 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Date from memory; see the footer.

    A football is one ball of pentagons and hexagons. What about a far bigger one, like a dome or a virus shell?

    Chapter 8

    Domes and virus shells follow the same rule

    They are the same ball, only finer. Hexagons by the hundred, pentagons still 12.

    A geodesic dome is a ball made of triangles, like a climbing frame. Start with the icosahedron from Chapter 1. Cut each triangle into smaller triangles and push the new corners out to the ball. Then swap: turn every corner into a patch. Most corners have six triangles round them and become hexagons. The twelve original corners have five and become pentagons.

    Left button: the dome of triangles. Right button: its patch version. Drag the slider to cut finer.

    A few things to notice

    • Set “Finer” to 1 and press “shell”. That is the dodecahedron: 12 pentagons, no hexagons at all.
    • Slide to 3. The shell has 80 hexagons. Still 12 pentagons, and the number still reads 2.
    • Press “dome”, then “shell”. The shell's corners equal the dome's triangles, its patches equal the dome's corners, and the edges match.

    Take this with you

    You can make the ball as fine as you like. The rule holds at every size, and the pentagons never change: 12.

    Side note: domes and viruses (from the history books)

    Buckminster Fuller built domes like this in the 1950s. Many viruses build their protein coat the same way, with twelve five-fold spots and the rest six-fold. Those are facts to look up, not ones this page can show you. What the page can show is why any such ball must carry 12 pentagons.

    Every shape so far gave 2. Is there any shape that doesn't?

    Chapter 9

    A donut plays by a different rule

    Yes. A shape with a hole through it gives 0, and you can count it below.

    These shapes are built from building blocks, like sugar cubes glued together. Every little square face counts as a face of its own. First a plain block. Then a block with a dent in it. Then a picture frame, which is a square donut. Then a frame with two holes.

    Why does the hole matter? The squash step breaks. Pop one face of a donut and press: it won't lie flat. The ring still loops round the hole. You need a second cut through the ring, and that second cut costs the number 2. Two holes, two rings, two extra cuts: −2.

    A few things to notice

    • The dented block still gives 2. Dents don't matter. Only holes right through do.
    • Each hole costs exactly 2. One hole: 0. Two holes: −2.
    • The block made of eight cubes has 24 faces, not 6, yet still gives 2. Splitting faces is Chapter 5's move.

    Take this with you

    The rule is 2 for any shape you could squash flat after one pop. A hole stops the squash, and each hole takes 2 off.

    Side note: monster shapes that seem to break the rule

    Choose “two blocks touching along an edge” or “touching at a corner” in the menu. The count comes out wrong, and the sim says why. Those aren't one solid shape: the touching place is paper-thin. Choose “two separate blocks”: each is 2 on its own, so together they read 4. The rule promises 2 only for one solid piece without holes.

    Holes aside, the number is always 2, and you know why. Let's put the whole reason on one page.

    Chapter 10

    The whole argument fits on one page

    Here is everything, with the numbers live.

    Think of a recipe card. Four steps, and the dish is a proof. Choose a shape and press the steps in order. The line under the picture shows the running total.

    1 Count. Corners, edges, faces. 2 Squash. Pop a face, press flat; the outside is a face, so nothing changes. 3 Snip. Cut a line between two patches, or snip a loose dot. Each snip drops one adder and one take-away. 4 Dot. One corner, no lines, one patch: 1 − 0 + 1 = 2. So the shape gave 2.

    A few things to notice

    • Run the four steps on three different shapes. The ending line is identical every time.
    • Step 3 is the only one with any arithmetic in it, and it is only ever “up 1, down 1”.
    • Nothing in the steps asks which shape it is. That is why it is a proof.

    The ideas, and where you met them

    • Corners, edges, faces; an edge always has two faces beside it. Ch. 1, toothpicks and plasticine.
    • One number keeps turning up: 2. Ch. 2.
    • Squash flat; the outside is a face. Ch. 3, straws and pipe cleaners.
    • Snipping and growing can't move it. Ch. 4, pencil snip game; Ch. 5.
    • Checking versus proving. Ch. 6.
    • Twelve pentagons, domes, donuts. Ch. 7, a ball and stickers; Ch. 8; Ch. 9.

    Take this with you

    Count, squash, snip, dot. Four steps that work on any solid shape without a hole, which is why the answer is always 2.

    One thing left to do: make a shape nobody has ever named, and check it.

    Chapter 11

    Build a shape nobody has named

    Your turn.

    Below are three floors of a building site, each a 4 by 4 grid. Tap squares to put blocks down or take them away. The shape appears on the right, and the count updates as you go. Build something lumpy. Build a tower. Build a shape with an arch, or two.

    Floor 1 is the ground. Blocks must share a whole face with a neighbour to count as one solid shape.

    A few things to notice

    • Build an arch: a block bridge with a gap under it. The count drops to 0, because an arch is a hole.
    • Put two blocks corner to corner. The sim flags it: not one solid shape, so no promise.
    • Press “Surprise me” a few times. Lumpy, holed or not, you can tell the answer before reading it.

    Your shape may have a name now, but the rule never asked for one. It only asked whether the blocks make one solid piece, and whether anything passes right through.

    Here is the whole reason in one sentence. Pop, press flat, snip down to a dot: no step moves the number, and the dot shows 2.

    Take this with you

    You found the number, proved it, predicted the football with it, and built a shape to break it. Grown-ups call this number the Euler characteristic, and the study of what survives squashing and stretching is called topology. You just did some.

    What the sims really do. Every shape on these pages is stored as real 3D corners and faces. The counts are read off that geometry, never typed in. The flat maps are drawn by a method that spreads the shape out so no lines cross. The snip and grow moves recount the patches from the map each time. The building-site shapes count every unit square as a face and test whether the blocks form one solid piece.

    What they skip. “Allowed” shapes here are solid, in one piece, with flat faces and no holes through them, which is what the proof needs. The sims don't handle curved faces, shapes with faces that have holes in them, or shapes that pass through themselves. Two blocks touching only along an edge or at a corner are flagged rather than counted as one shape.
