Your farthest shot:

A short book with a cannon in it

Why doesn't the Moon fall down?

You throw a ball as hard as you can. It sails across the field. Then it lands. Everything you have ever thrown has landed.

Now look up. The Moon hangs there, night after night. Nobody is holding it. Nothing is under it. And it never lands.

This book finds out why. You will not be told the answer. You will fire a cannon until you find it.

Puzzles this book answers

Chapters

    Each chapter adds one idea and has something to poke. Use Next and Back, or the left and right arrow keys. The number in the top bar is your farthest shot so far. Watch what happens to it.

    A thrown ball falls just as fast as a dropped one

    Start with the ball that does land. It holds the first clue.

    Picture two friends on a high diving board. One just steps off the end. The other takes a running jump. Who hits the water first? Most people pick the one who steps off. The runner has farther to travel, after all.

    Let's test it with two balls on a cliff {{cliff}} metres high. Press the button. The blue ball is simply dropped. The orange ball is thrown sideways at the very same moment. Watch the two timers. Then change the throwing speed and go again. Try the gentlest throw, then the hardest.

    A few things to notice

    • Both timers stop on the same number, every single time.
    • At every moment the two balls are level with each other.
    • A harder throw changes where the orange ball lands, but never when.

    So the friends on the diving board hit the water together. Moving sideways and falling are two separate jobs. Gravity only does the falling. It pulls the thrown ball down exactly as it pulls the dropped one.

    Take this with you

    Going sideways does not slow a fall. A thrown ball and a dropped ball fall side by side.

    So both balls fall together. But how far do they fall in the first second?

    Extras: try it at home

    Two coins, one click

    Lay a ruler on a table with one end poking over the edge. Rest a coin on that end. Put a second coin on the table edge, beside the ruler. Flick the ruler sideways so it swats that coin away. The coin on the ruler is left behind and drops straight down. Listen for one click, not two.

    Why it works: both coins fall the same height together, like the two balls in this chapter's cliff sim.

    Everything falls about {{fall1r}} metres in its first second

    About {{fall1r}} metres in the first second, and you can measure it yourself.

    Sports photographers have a trick. They flash a light many times on one picture. A single diver shows up again and again, frozen in mid-air. We can do that with a ball.

    Throw the ball off the cliff. The sim freezes a copy of it at every tick of the clock. Dashed lines show how far each copy has fallen. Start with a slow throw. Now change the sideways speed and throw again. The old copies stay behind as ghosts. Do the new ones sit higher, lower, or on the same lines?

    A few things to notice

    • After one second the ball is {{fall1}} metres down. Call it {{fall1r}}.
    • A faster throw slides the copies sideways, never up or down.
    • After two seconds it is {{fall2r}} metres down, not {{twice5}}. Falling speeds up.

    The first second is the one to remember. A dropped ball, a thrown ball, a bullet: each falls the same {{fall1r}} metres in it. That number will matter a lot later.

    Take this with you

    Near the ground, everything falls about {{fall1r}} metres in its first second. Sideways speed makes no difference.

    If the fall is always the same, what does a harder throw actually buy you?

    Extras: what the sim leaves out, and a hammer on the Moon

    This sim has no air. Air holds a feather up, though it barely slows a ball. Galileo argued, about four hundred years ago, that without air everything would fall together.

    In 1971 an astronaut on the Moon tried it. He dropped a hammer and a feather. There is no air there. They landed together.

    Throw harder and it lands farther, but not later

    It buys distance, and nothing else.

    Roll a toy car off a table. Fast or slow, the drop to the floor takes the same time. A faster car simply gets farther before that time runs out.

    Back to the cliff. Throw several balls at different speeds. Each path stays on the picture, and each throw goes into the log. Before you throw, guess the landing spot. Your guesses will soon get good. Here is a way to guess well. The fall always takes {{cliffT}} seconds. So multiply the speed by {{cliffT}}. At {{exV}} metres a second that makes {{exD}} metres. Check one row of the log with your own multiplying.

    A few things to notice

    • Every throw is in the air for the same {{cliffT}} seconds.
    • Double the speed and the ball lands twice as far away.
    • Here the ground is flat, so every throw has to land somewhere.

    Think of the fall time as a budget. Height sets the budget. Speed decides how much ground you cover before it is spent. On this cliff, no throw can ever stay up longer.

    Take this with you

    Height sets how long a ball flies. Sideways speed sets how far it gets in that time.

    All of this assumed the ground is flat. Is it?

    Extras: for older readers

    The sim does not use the multiplying shortcut. It moves the ball one tiny step at a time. The shortcut and the steps agree in every row. That is a check, not a proof.

    The ground is not flat: it curves away from a straight ruler

    No. The ground is the outside of a giant ball.

    Watch a ship sail away on a clear day. Its hull disappears first, and its mast last. The sea bulges up between you and the ship. The ground does the same thing, too gently to notice.

    So let's measure the bend. Lay a perfectly straight ruler flat on the ground, and make it longer and longer. The ruler stays straight. The ground curves away beneath its far end. Move the slider and read the gap. The top picture is true to scale. The bottom one is stretched upward so the gap shows.

    A few things to notice

    • With a ruler {{kmFor}} kilometres long, the gap is {{gap8}} metres.
    • Double the ruler and the gap grows four times, not two.
    • Under a short ruler the gap is tiny. That is why the ground looks flat.

    You have met {{gap8r}} metres before. It is how far a ball falls in one second. Keep the two numbers side by side.

    Hold on to this idea

    {{gap8r}} metres, and {{kmFor}} kilometres. Travel {{kmFor}} kilometres dead straight, and the ground ends up {{gap8r}} metres below you.

    Take this with you

    The Earth curves away from every straight line. It drops about {{gap8r}} metres in {{kmFor}} kilometres.

    A fast ball tries to go straight. What happens when the ground curves away beneath it?

    Extras: for older readers

    The gap comes from a triangle with one square corner. Its sides are the ruler, the distance from the Earth's centre to the ground, and that distance plus the gap. The sim uses that triangle exactly.

    What it leaves out: hills, valleys, and the Earth being very slightly squashed.

    From a tall mountain, each faster shot lands farther round the curve

    It lands farther away than it would on flat ground. To see that, we need a serious cannon.

    And serious speed. A passenger jet covers about {{plane}} kilometres every second. Our cannon deals in whole kilometres a second.

    Isaac Newton drew this very cannon. His drawing was printed in 1728, the year after he died. He put it on a mountain far too tall to be real. Ours is {{mount}} kilometres high, above nearly all the air. So nothing slows the ball down.

    Fire at {{s1}}, then {{s2}}, then {{s3}} kilometres a second. The page logs every shot. Then try faster ones. On flat ground, doubling the speed doubles the distance. Is that still true here? Use your log to decide. The two buttons are there if you get stuck.

    A few things to notice

    • Each shot lands farther round the curve than the one before.
    • The camera has to pull back to keep the landing in view.
    • Try a slow pair and a fast pair. Does doubling work the same for both?

    The ball falls just as it did off the cliff. But now the ground curves away while it falls. So the ball has farther to drop, and more time to travel.

    Take this with you

    On a round Earth the ground curves away under a fast ball. That buys it extra time, and extra distance.

    Each faster shot lands farther round. Could one go so far round that it never lands?

    Extras: what this cannon leaves out

    Air, which would slow a real ball and burn it up. The Earth's spin. And the mountain itself: the tallest real one is not a tenth as high.

    At one speed the ball never lands

    Yes. And you are about to find that shot yourself.

    Imagine walking down a staircase that sinks as fast as you step. You would keep stepping down, and never reach the floor.

    Same cannon, same mountain. Your old shots are still in the log. The slider now goes higher, in finer steps. Hunt for the slowest shot that does not land. You will know when you have it. One speed lands. The very next one keeps going.

    The rest of this chapter opens when your log shows two next-door speeds: one that lands, and one that does not.

    You found it: about {{vFoundR}} kilometres a second. Now remember the idea you were holding. It was {{gap8r}} metres, and {{kmFor}} kilometres.

    In one second your ball goes {{vFound}} km sideways.

    In that second it falls {{fallM}} m.

    Over those {{vFound}} km the round Earth curves away {{curveM}} m.

    So after one second the ball is still {{mount}} km up. It is no closer to the ground. The next second goes the same way, and the one after that.

    One step slower, at {{vBelow}} km a second, the ball loses a little height each second. It landed {{belowDist}} away.

    The ball never stops falling. The ground just keeps curving out of its way. A fall like that, all the way round, has a name. It is called an orbit.

    A few things to notice

    • The ball comes back to the mountain at the height it left.
    • Nothing pushes it along. There is no engine and no fuel.
    • Just below this speed, the ball still lands, a long way round.

    Look at the log once more. Slow shots fell short. Faster ones landed farther round. This one just runs out of ground to land on.

    Take this with you

    An orbit is a fall that never reaches the ground. Each second the ball falls about {{gap8r}} metres, and the ground curves about {{gap8r}} metres away.

    A good place to stop for the night. Your ball is in orbit.

    Extras: for older readers, and the first real one

    Why do the two numbers match in every second, not just the first? Showing that takes algebra. Here you can only watch lap after lap. Checking is not the same as proving.

    The first real cannonball that stayed up was Sputnik, in October 1957. A rocket did the work of the mountain and the cannon.

    Faster still, the circle stretches into an oval, and then the ball escapes

    The ball swings out higher before it comes back. The circle stretches.

    Think of a playground swing. A gentle push gives a small arc. A big push carries you far out before you swing back. Now imagine a push so big that you never swing back at all.

    Your cannon now reaches {{vmax}} kilometres a second. Your earlier shots are still in the log. Start a little above your orbit speed. Then creep upward, one shot at a time. Each loop is longer than the last. The camera will pull back to fit it. Keep going, and watch for the shot that does not return.

    A few things to notice

    • Every loop is a stretched circle, an oval, and it closes at the mountain.
    • The ball races past the Earth and crawls at the far end.
    • Past one speed the ball never turns back. Your log shows which.

    Why does it slow down far out? Climbing away from the Earth costs speed, like a ball thrown straight up. A fast enough ball never runs out. Gravity keeps tugging, but ever more weakly, and the ball gets away.

    Take this with you

    Faster than orbit speed, the path is an oval. Faster than about {{vEscR}} kilometres a second, the ball leaves for good.

    People live up there in orbit, and they float. Why?

    Extras: for older readers

    The ovals have a proper name: ellipses. Newton proved that gravity's rule makes exact ellipses. That proof needs maths you will meet much later. The sim only shows that each oval closes up. Checking is not proving.

    A fun one to fire: at {{v11}} kilometres a second the ball swings out {{ap11}} km. That is most of the way to the Moon.

    Astronauts float because they are falling

    Because they are falling, not because gravity has gone. Up there it is nearly as strong as down here.

    Jump on a trampoline. At the top of each bounce your tummy goes light. For that moment you and your clothes are all falling together. Nothing presses on anything.

    The Space Station flies about {{iss}} kilometres up. It laps the Earth in about {{issLap}} minutes. Raise the cannon to that height and launch the station. The little window shows an astronaut inside it. Watch the gap between astronaut and wall. Then press Stop it dead, and see what gravity does next.

    A few things to notice

    • At {{iss}} kilometres up, gravity is still {{issG}} percent as strong. That is nearly nine-tenths.
    • The astronaut and the station fall together, so the gap between them holds.
    • Take away the sideways speed and the station drops like a stone.

    On the ground, the floor pushes up on your feet. That push is what you feel as weight. In orbit the floor is falling too. It cannot push. So the astronaut floats, with gravity pulling the whole time.

    Take this with you

    Floating in orbit does not mean gravity is missing. It means falling together with everything around you.

    The station orbits a touch slower than your cannonball did. Why would higher mean slower?

    Extras: try it at home

    The leaky cup

    Poke two holes near the bottom of a paper cup. Fill it with water outdoors. Two jets squirt out. Now drop the cup. While it falls, the jets stop.

    Why it works: the water and the cup fall together, so the water stops pressing on the holes. It is the astronaut in this chapter's little window.

    Higher up, gravity is weaker, so you can go slower

    Because gravity is weaker up there. The station falls a little less each second. So it needs less speed to keep missing the ground.

    Sit close to a campfire and it roasts you. Shuffle back and the warmth fades. The Earth's pull fades with distance in much the same way.

    Slide the cannon up and down. At each height the sim finds the speed that just orbits. It also times one lap. Press Add to my table at five or six different heights. Go really, really high. Then run your eye down each column. What goes up? What goes down?

    A few things to notice

    • Each column only ever goes one way as you climb.
    • Your highest row is a slow, far-off lap. Remember it.
    • At {{twoH}} kilometres up you are twice as far from the Earth's centre. Gravity is a quarter.

    That last one is the rule. Twice as far from the centre, gravity is four times weaker. Three times as far, nine times weaker. You can check it in the sim: at about 12,700 kilometres up you are three times as far, and gravity is about a ninth. Checking is not proving, but it is a good start.

    Take this with you

    Far from the Earth, gravity is gentle. A slow ball can orbit there, and each lap takes a long time.

    Is anything really out there, far away, orbiting slowly?

    Extras: for older readers

    Why four times weaker at twice the distance? Picture the pull spreading out like light from a lamp. Twice as far away, the same light covers four times the area. So each patch gets a quarter. That is a picture, not a proof.

    The Moon is a cannonball

    Yes. You have seen it all your life. It is the Moon.

    Think of an apple. From its core to its skin is one distance, all the way round. For the Earth, that centre-to-ground distance is called its radius. It is {{re}} kilometres.

    The Moon is {{moonR}} of those radii away. Radii means more than one radius. Out there gravity is about {{moonWeak}} times weaker than on the ground. That is 60 times 60, the rule from the last chapter. Use the zoom slider to pull back from your cannon to the Moon. Nothing else changes. It is the same Earth and the same rule for gravity.

    A few things to notice

    • The Moon moves at about {{moonVr}} kilometre a second and takes about {{moonDays}} days a lap.
    • Each second it falls just over {{moonFallR}} millimetre. Its huge circle curves away by the same amount.
    • True to scale, the Earth and Moon are specks in a lot of empty space.

    So the Moon does fall. It falls {{moonFall}} millimetres, every second, toward the Earth. But it also slides {{moonVr}} kilometre sideways in that second. Fall and curve match, just as they did for your cannonball. It gets no closer.

    Take this with you

    The Moon is a cannonball, a long way up and going slowly. It falls all the time, and it always misses.

    Gravity bends the Moon's path into a circle. What if gravity let go?

    Extras: an apple, and what the sim leaves out

    The story goes that Newton watched an apple fall, in about 1666. He wondered whether the same pull reached the Moon.

    This sim leaves out three things. The Moon pulls back on the Earth. The Sun tugs on both. And the Moon's real path is slightly oval. So the real lap is a few hours shorter.

    Without gravity the Moon would leave in a straight line

    It would stop curving and sail away.

    Slide a puck across smooth ice. It keeps going, dead straight, long after your push has ended. Moving things do not need a push to keep moving. They only need one to speed up, slow down or turn.

    The Moon is the same. Nothing pushes it along. Gravity only pulls it off its straight line, toward the Earth, second after second. Flip the switch and turn gravity off. Leave it off for a few days. Watch the Moon's trail against the dashed line. Then switch gravity back on, and see what it does.

    A few things to notice

    • With gravity off, the Moon keeps its speed and goes perfectly straight.
    • It does not drop toward the Earth. It does not stop either.
    • Switch gravity back on soon and the path bends again, into a new loop.

    So there are two halves to an orbit. One is the sideways speed, which the Moon keeps for free. The other is gravity, which keeps bending that straight line into a circle. Take away the speed and the Moon falls. Take away gravity and the Moon leaves.

    Take this with you

    Moving things go straight unless something pulls them. Gravity is the pull that turns the Moon's straight line into a circle.

    Can you put the whole story together now?

    Ball on a string

    Outdoors, swing a soft ball round on a string, then let go. It does not fly straight outward. It leaves along the line it was moving on, like the Moon in this chapter's switch sim.

    Card and coin

    Rest a card on a glass with a coin on top. Flick the card away fast. The coin stays put, then drops into the glass. Still things stay still unless something pulls them, too.

    An orbit is a fall that never reaches the ground

    Yes. Here is the whole argument, with your own numbers in it.

    A thrown ball falls as fast as a dropped one: {{fall1}} m in its first second.

    The ground curves away from a straight line: {{gap8}} m down in {{kmFor}} km.

    Your cannon, {{mount}} km up: the ball landed at {{vBelow}} km a second and never landed at {{vFound}}.

    At that speed, each second, it goes {{vFound}} km sideways, falls {{fallM}} m, and the Earth curves away {{curveM}} m.

    The Moon, {{moonR}} radii out, does the same thing slowly: {{moonV}} km a second, {{moonDays}} days a lap. Each second it falls {{moonFall}} mm and its circle curves away {{moonCurve}} mm.

    Things to try at home:

    Make your own: park a satellite

    A TV dish on a wall points at one spot in the sky. It never moves. So the thing it points at must hover over one place on the ground. That thing is a satellite: a cannonball someone put in orbit. To hover, it must go round as fast as the Earth turns. That is one lap a day. Low orbits are too quick. The Moon is far too slow. Somewhere between is a height that works. Find it.

    A few things to notice

    • Too low, and the satellite races ahead of the house.
    • Too high, and it drifts behind.
    • At the right height it sits on the dashed line all day.

    Take this with you

    An orbit needs no engine. It needs sideways speed, and gravity to bend it.

    The Moon never lands because, as fast as it falls, its sideways speed carries it round the Earth.

    Extras: for older readers

    Real TV satellites match the Earth's true spin. One turn takes about {{sid}} minutes less than a day, so they sit a little lower than your answer.
