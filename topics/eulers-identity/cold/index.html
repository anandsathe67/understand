Let's Walk Round a Circle

How a bank, a turning arrow and a piece of string explain the strangest-looking equation on any T-shirt.

My cousin Riya came to lunch wearing a black T-shirt with white writing. It said eiπ + 1 = 0, and nothing else. "What does it mean?" I asked. She said, "It's the most beautiful equation in maths." "But what does it mean?" She chewed her roti and said, "It links the five most important numbers." That is true, and it is also the least useful sentence anyone has ever said about it. It's like saying a bicycle links a saddle and two wheels.

This page is the answer I wish Riya had given me. We are going to build the whole thing out of four ordinary ideas: multiplying, a number line, turning, and doing the same small thing many, many times. There is no step where you have to "just trust me". Where a step is too hard for a ten-year-old to carry alone, I say so and mark it For older readers or Parent, carry here.

By the end you'll be able to answer:

  • How can a number be imaginary? Isn't that cheating?
  • How can multiplying ever make something spin?
  • Why does the number e show up both in bank accounts and in circles?
  • What could "e to the power of a turn" possibly mean? You can't multiply something by itself a turn times.
  • Why π and not 180? Who said circles are measured in π?

It takes two or three sittings. There are two "good place to stop" signs along the way. Every picture is a real calculation running live in your browser, so drag, press and slide everything.

Section 1
A power is a multiplication done again and again

Suppose you have a photocopier that doubles things. You put in 1 sweet, you get 2. Put the 2 back in: 4. Again: 8. Again: 16. Doubling four times in a row takes 1 to 16.

Writing "2 × 2 × 2 × 2" is long. Mathematicians write it with a tiny raised number: 24. It just means "multiply by 2, four times". 23 means 2 × 2 × 2 = 8. 102 means 10 × 10 = 100. The raised number is called a power (or an exponent), and the whole page is about one of them, so let's make sure it feels like a machine you can run.

Multiply by
The power machine. Pick a number, pick how many times to multiply by it, and the result is computed live.

A few things to notice

  • Set "how many times" to 0. The answer is 1 every time. Zero multiplications leaves the starting 1 alone.
  • Try 1.1, twelve times. A small push, repeated, adds up: 1 becomes about 3.1.
  • Try ½. Multiplying can also shrink things. Hold on to that.

The little raised number was made popular by René Descartes in his book La Géométrie in 1637. Before that, people wrote things like "the cube of the number", in words.

Hold on to this idea

A power is a machine run many times. Everything that follows is: pick one small step, and repeat it.

Side remark for older readers: when a grown-up writes 2n, the letter n is just a space where a number like 4 or 12 will go. "Multiply by 2, n times."

Section 2
Multiplying stretches an arrow

Draw a number line on the floor: 0 in the middle, 1, 2, 3 going right, −1, −2, −3 going left. Now instead of thinking of a number as a dot on the line, think of it as an arrow from 0 to the dot. The number 3 is an arrow three steps long pointing right.

What does "multiply by 2" do to the arrow? It stretches it to twice the length. Multiply by ½ and it shrinks to half. The arrow never turns; it only gets longer or shorter.

An arrow on the number line. Each button multiplies the arrow by that number. The line re-scales itself when the arrow gets long.

A few things to notice

  • Press × 2 three times: 1 → 2 → 4 → 8. That's the power machine from Section 1, drawn as stretching.
  • Press × 2 and then × ½. You're back where you started. Shrinking undoes stretching.
  • Notice what never happens: the arrow never points left. No amount of stretching does that.

Section 3
Multiplying by −1 is a half-turn

Now press × (−1) below. The arrow 3 becomes −3. It could have got there by jumping across. But watch: the sim turns the arrow, like the hand of a clock, through half a turn, from pointing right to pointing left. Length stays the same. Only the direction changes.

Is that cheating? Here's the test. Everybody learns the rule "negative times negative is positive" and nobody is told why. If × (−1) is a half-turn, then × (−1) twice is two half-turns, which is one whole turn, which is back where you started. (−1) × (−1) = 1. The rule isn't a rule at all; it's what two half-turns do.

The same number-line arrow, with a new button. × (−1) swings the arrow through a half-turn (180°). Nothing else about it changes.

A few things to notice

  • Press × (−1) twice. You get back to 3. Two half-turns is a full turn.
  • Press × 2 then × (−1): the arrow is −6. Press × (−1) then × 2: also −6. Stretching and turning don't care which order you do them.
  • Multiplying by −2 would be "stretch by 2 and half-turn", both at once.

Hold on to this idea

Multiplying an arrow does two things: it stretches it (or shrinks it) and it turns it. So far the only turn we've seen is a half-turn. Keep your eye on the turning.

Section 4
A quarter-turn, done twice, is a half-turn. We call it i

Here is a question. × (−1) is a half-turn. Is there a number which, when you multiply by it twice, gives a half-turn? Something that, done twice, is × (−1)?

Think about turning. What turn, done twice, is a half-turn? A quarter-turn, obviously: turn a quarter, turn another quarter, and you've turned half. So the number we want is "multiply = quarter-turn". There's one problem: a quarter-turn makes the arrow point up, and there is no "up" on a number line.

So we give ourselves an up. Draw a second number line through 0, going up and down. Now the arrow has a whole flat plane to point in. The quarter-turn number is so useful it gets its own letter: i. An arrow of length 1 pointing straight up is called i. An arrow of length 2 pointing up is 2i. Multiplying by i means "turn a quarter-turn, don't stretch".

The number plane. Across is the old number line; up-and-down is the new one. × i is a quarter-turn anticlockwise; × (−i) is a quarter-turn clockwise.

A few things to notice

  • Reset, then press × i twice. The arrow is at −1. Two quarter-turns is a half-turn: i × i = −1. That's the whole secret of i.
  • Press × i four times. Back to 1. Four quarter-turns is a full turn.
  • Press × 2 then × i. The arrow is 2i: two up. Multiplying by a plain number stretches; multiplying by i turns; they don't fight.
  • Press × (−i) then × i. Back where you were. Clockwise undoes anticlockwise.

Now the first puzzle. "How can a number be imaginary?" It isn't, really. i is no more imaginary than −1. −1 means "one step, but turned round", and i means "one step, but turned a quarter". Nobody panics about −1. The word "imaginary" is a 400-year-old insult that stuck.

Descartes used the word "imaginary" in 1637 as a put-down: he meant these numbers were made-up and useless. For 150 years nobody drew them. Then a Norwegian surveyor, Caspar Wessel, drew the number plane in a paper presented in 1797; a Paris bookkeeper, Jean-Robert Argand, drew it again in 1806; and Carl Friedrich Gauss used it and gave the numbers the name "complex" in 1831. Once people could see i as a direction, the insult stopped making sense.

Try it at home · the paper arrow

Cut an arrow out of paper and put a pin or a dot of blu-tack through its tail. Point it right. Say "times i" and turn it a quarter anticlockwise: it points up. Say it again: it points left, which is −1. Again: down, which is −i. Again: right, home.

Why it works: the paper arrow is exactly the arrow in the plane sim above, and your hand is the multiplication. Count the quarter-turns: two of them always make a half-turn, no matter where you start. That is i × i = −1 in your fingers.

A good place to stop

You now have the whole number plane and you know what i is. That's a full sitting. Next time: every multiplication is a stretch plus a turn, and then a bank.

Section 5
Every multiplication is a stretch and a turn

So far we can stretch (× 2), half-turn (× −1) and quarter-turn (× i). What about a small turn, say an eighth of a turn? There must be a number for that too, and there is. Here is how to find it with a rule you already know.

You know that 4 × (10 + 2) is 40 + 8. Multiplying by "10 plus 2" means "multiply by 10, multiply by 2, add the results". The same works for arrows. Multiplying an arrow by "1 + i" means: take one copy of the arrow (that's × 1), take one copy turned a quarter (that's × i), and add them tip-to-tail. One step forward plus one step sideways is a diagonal: longer by about 1.41×, turned by 45°, an eighth of a turn. The sim draws both copies so you can see them join.

Parent, carry here

The step "multiply by (1 + i) = one copy plus one turned copy" is the ordinary rule for multiplying out a sum, the one your child uses for 4 × 12 = 40 + 8. The new thing is only that one of the pieces is a turn. If they accept "× i is a quarter-turn" and "adding arrows is tip-to-tail", they have everything. Let the sim do the drawing; don't make them do the algebra.

Drag the orange dot to choose any multiplier. Blue is your arrow. The dashed pieces are "a copy of the arrow" and "a copy turned a quarter", added tip-to-tail. Red is where they end up: the arrow times the multiplier.

A few things to notice

  • With the multiplier at 1 + i, press "Multiply the arrow" twice. The arrow goes 1 → (1 + i) → 2i: straight up, length 2. Two eighth-turns made a quarter-turn, and 1.41 × 1.41 = 2. So (1 + i) × (1 + i) = 2i, checked by watching.
  • Press the 1 + 0.1i preset. The readout says it stretches by only 1.005× and turns by 5.7°. A small sideways slice is almost a pure turn, with hardly any stretch.
  • Drag the orange dot anywhere. Whatever you pick, the readout always says "stretch by so much, turn by so much". Every multiplication is a stretch and a turn. There are no other kinds.

For older readers · the brackets

(1 + i)(1 + i) = 1 + i + i + i·i = 1 + 2i − 1 = 2i, using i·i = −1 from Section 4. The stretch factor of 1 + i is its length, √(1² + 1²) = √2 ≈ 1.414, and its turn is 45° because it points along the diagonal. In general, multiplying by a + bi stretches by √(a² + b²) and turns by the angle of the point (a, b).

Hold on to this idea

Adding a small sideways slice to an arrow turns it a little and hardly stretches it at all. We'll come back to this with a vengeance in Section 7.

Section 6
Growing in tiny slices lands on e

Now a completely different story, which is true. Imagine a bank that pays 100% interest a year. You put in ₹100; after a year the bank adds 100% of it, and you have ₹200. Doubling. Fine.

A friendlier bank says: "We'll pay the same 100%, but in two halves. After six months we add 50%, and after another six months we add 50% of whatever you have then." ₹100 → ₹150 → ₹225. More! Because the second 50% was paid on the first 50% too.

So you ask for monthly: twelve slices of 1/12 each. Then daily: 365 slices of 1/365. Then every second. Surely with enough slices you get rich without limit?

You don't. Slide and watch. The money creeps up and then stops creeping, at about ₹271.83. More slices don't help. That stopping number, 2.71828…, is called e. It is what "growing by 100% in tiny, tiny slices" adds up to.

The growth machine. The staircase is your money through one year; each step is one interest payment of (growth ÷ number of slices). The dotted line is where the staircase is heading as the slices get tinier.

A few things to notice

  • Paid once: ₹200. Twice: ₹225. Monthly: ₹261.30. Daily: ₹271.46. Every second: ₹271.83. The jumps get smaller and smaller; the machine is settling, not exploding.
  • In Section 1 notation, "monthly" is the power machine: (1 + 1/12) multiplied 12 times. That's (1 + 1/12)12. e is this power machine with the slices made as small as you like.
  • Now set "total growth" to 2 and slide to every second. ₹100 becomes ₹738.91. And 2.718 × 2.718 = 7.389. Growing by 200% in tiny slices is the same as growing by 100% in tiny slices, twice. Try 3: ₹2008.55 = 2.718 × 2.718 × 2.718.

That last bullet matters. Feed the machine growth 2 and you get e × e, which is e2. Feed it 3 and you get e3. So the machine's answer for growth of "some amount" is always "e to the power of that amount". That is why the T-shirt has an e with a power on it. The power is the amount you fed the machine.

Jacob Bernoulli asked exactly this bank question in 1683 and found the answer was stuck between 2 and 3, but he never found its digits. Leonhard Euler worked out those digits (2.71828182845…) and began calling the number e in a manuscript around 1727; the letter first appeared in print in his book Mechanica in 1736. Nobody knows for sure why he picked e. It is probably not for "Euler": he was a modest man and the letters a, b, c, d were already busy.

Try it at home · the calculator race

On any calculator, work out 1.01 × 1.01 × 1.01… a hundred times (most calculators let you press = repeatedly after typing × 1.01; or use the power button: 1.01 xy 100). You get 2.7048. Now race it against 1.001 to the power 1000: 2.7169. And 1.0001 to the power 10,000: 2.7181. Smaller slice, more slices, closer to 2.71828.

Why it works: 1.01 a hundred times is the bank paying 1% a hundred times, which is 100% in a hundred slices. It is the growth-machine sim with the slider at 100, and your calculator is doing the same multiplication the sim does.

Hold on to this idea

The growth machine is: keep what you have, and add a small slice of it. Repeat. Fed "100%" in tiny slices it lands on e; fed "200%" it lands on e × e. Now ask the obvious silly question: what if the slice we add isn't "more of the same", but sideways?

A good place to stop

You've met e and you know what it is: the settling point of growth in tiny slices. Next sitting is the big one: we feed the same machine a turn.

Section 7
Turning in tiny slices walks you round a circle

Section 5 showed that adding a small sideways slice to an arrow turns it a little. Section 6 showed a machine that adds a small slice many times. Put them together.

Start with the arrow 1. Instead of adding "a slice of the arrow" (which stretches it, like the bank), add "a slice of the arrow turned a quarter", which is a sideways slice. Let's make the total of all the slices add up to 3.14 arrow-lengths, which is π. Why π? Patience: it's a guess for now, and the next section explains it. With 1 slice, you add one big sideways step of length 3.14. With 10 slices, ten sideways steps of 0.314 each, and each one is sideways to the arrow as it is then, which has already turned a bit. Run it.

The turn machine. Each short segment is one sideways slice, added to the arrow's tip. The chain is where the tip goes; the thick arrow is the final arrow. The target, −1, is marked.

A few things to notice

  • One slice: a single sideways step of 3.14. The arrow ends up far away, up at (1, 3.14). Nothing like −1.
  • Ten slices (the default): the chain is already bending into a curve, and the arrow has turned 174°, almost a half-turn. But it's too long, 1.60 instead of 1, so it lands at (−1.59, 0.16), past the target.
  • Slide up. 100 slices: 179.9°, length 1.05. 1000 slices: 180.0°, length 1.005, landing at (−1.005, 0.000). 100,000 slices: length 1.00005. The landing creeps onto −1 and stays there. Nobody told it to; it just does.
  • Press Play with 10 or 20 slices and watch the tip walk. Every step is sideways to the arrow at that moment, so the tip keeps curling. That curling is the circle.

This is the moment the whole page was built for. We never said "circle" anywhere. We said "add a sideways slice, again and again", and a circle appeared on its own, and the arrow walked exactly halfway round it, onto −1.

Why does sideways make a circle? Whirl a stone on a string. At every instant the stone is pulled sideways to the way it's going, and it goes round in a circle. A sideways push never makes you go faster or slower; it only changes which way you're facing. Our slices are sideways pushes on the arrow's tip. So the tip can't get much longer; it can only turn.

Parent, carry here

Two things to hold for the child. First, "sideways to the arrow now" is the key phrase: the direction of each slice keeps changing, which is why the path curls rather than running off in a straight line. Second, the small overshoot (length 1.60 at 10 slices, 1.005 at 1000) is real, not a drawing error, and it shrinks as the slices get smaller. A sideways slice of length 0.314 doesn't just turn the arrow; it also makes it a hair longer, like the 1.005× from Section 5. Ten hairs add up to 60% extra; a thousand tinier hairs add up to only 0.5%. The box below has the arithmetic.

For older readers · why the overshoot shrinks

Each step multiplies the arrow by (1 + iπ/n). Its length is √(1 + π²/n²), which for large n is about 1 + π²/(2n²). Multiplying n such lengths gives about 1 + π²/(2n). For n = 1000 that is 1.0049, matching the readout. As n → ∞ it goes to 1: the stretch disappears and only the turn remains. The turn per step is arctan(π/n), which for large n is π/n, so n steps turn by π in total. Both facts together say the chain converges to a half-circle of radius 1 ending at −1. (If you bent each step to stay exactly on the circle instead of going straight, you would land on −1 for every n.)

Try it at home · the matchstick chain

Take 12 matchsticks (or 12 equal strips of paper). Lay the first one flat. Lay the second one touching its end, turned by one hour on a clock face (30°). Lay the third turned another 30° from the second. Keep going. After 6 sticks you've turned 180° and you're pointing back the way you came, halfway round a rough circle. After 12 you've closed the loop.

Why it works: each stick is one slice in the turn-machine sim, pointing sideways to the arrow from the centre. Six slices of 30° is a half-turn. Your rough circle has corners because 12 slices is few; the sim with 100 slices has corners too, just too small to see.

Section 8
π radius-lengths of string reach exactly halfway round

We fed the machine a total of 3.14 arrow-lengths of sideways slices, and the arrow ended up halfway round. Why 3.14?

Because the tip of the arrow was walking along the circle, and the slices were its steps. So "how far did it turn" is really "how much string did it walk". And there's a fact about circles that every ten-year-old has heard but few have felt: the distance all the way round a circle is 2π times the radius, about 6.28 radius-lengths. Halfway round is 3.14 radius-lengths. Our arrow has length 1, so the circle it walks has radius 1, so halfway round is exactly π of string.

A circle of radius 1 and a piece of string. The straight string (below) has a tick every radius-length. The same string is wrapped round the circle (red), and the arrow points to where it ends.

A few things to notice

  • One radius-length of string wraps to an angle of 57.3°. That angle has a name: one radian. It is the circle's own natural unit of turn, measured by string, not by someone's choice of 360.
  • Press π. The arrow lands exactly on −1. Press 2π: all the way round, back to 1.
  • The second puzzle from the top of the page: "why π and not 180?" Because the machine doesn't know about degrees. It only knows how much sideways string it added. 180 is a human unit; π is the string's unit.

Side remark for older readers: grown-ups write the amount of string as θ (theta, a Greek letter) and say "an angle of θ radians". It's just the slider position above.

Roger Cotes, a young Cambridge mathematician, was measuring angles by string length around 1714, three decades before Euler's famous book. The word "radian" is much younger: James Thomson, a professor in Belfast, coined it in 1873.

Try it at home · the plate and the string

Find a round steel plate (a thali) or a roti. Measure from the centre to the edge with a piece of string and cut the string to that length: one radius. Cut six of these, and one extra cut to about 0.14 of a radius (a little bit; for a 10 cm radius, about 1.4 cm). Now lay radius-strings end to end around the edge: 1, 2, 3, and the little bit. You're at exactly the far side, halfway round. Three more radii and the little bit again brings you home: 6.28 radii, 2π.

Why it works: this is the string sim with your hands. The plate's edge is the circle of Section 7, the strings are the slices, and π is just the number of radius-lengths it takes to go halfway. It isn't a fact about the number 3.14; it's a fact about round things.

Section 9
Run the machine with a turn of π and you land on −1

Now we can say the whole thing in words, with no symbols at all.

The bank machine keeps your money and adds a slice of it. Run it with a growth of 1 in tiny slices and your money becomes e times bigger. The turn machine keeps your arrow and adds a sideways slice of it. Run it with a turn of π in tiny slices and your arrow walks halfway round a circle, from 1 to −1.

It's the same machine. The only difference is the slice. A straight slice is "more of the same direction" (growth). A sideways slice is "a quarter-turn of the arrow". That answers the third puzzle. Why does e show up in bank accounts and in circles? Because it is one machine. Fed a straight slice, it makes e. Fed a sideways slice, it makes a circle.

Now the naming. In Section 6 the machine fed growth 2 gave e2, and fed growth 3 gave e3. The machine's answer is always "e to the power of what you fed it". A sideways slice of π is written iπ. (That's π turned a quarter, which is what multiplying by i does.) So when the machine is fed iπ, mathematicians write its answer as eiπ, to match. And we've watched where it lands:

eiπ = −1

Then add 1 to both sides. The arrow at −1, plus one step to the right, is at 0, home:

eiπ + 1 = 0

Read it as a sentence: "Walk halfway round the circle, take one step right, and you're back at the start." That is what Riya's T-shirt says.

Parent, carry here · the honest bit about the name

Your child may object: "You can't multiply e by itself iπ times. That's not a number of times." They're right, and the page shouldn't pretend otherwise. eiπ is a name given to the machine's output. For ordinary numbers the machine's output really is a power of e. It would be strange to switch names the moment the slice turns sideways. This naming is also the only one that keeps the usual rules of powers working. Older readers can check: feed the machine iπ twice in a row, and it lands where "fed 2iπ" lands. That is exactly what ea·eb = ea+b demands. But it is a definition, not a discovery. The discovery is where the machine lands.

The turn machine fed any amount of turn. The grey dot is the string-sim answer from Section 8 (walk that far along the circle). The red arrow is where the slice machine actually lands.

A few things to notice

  • At 250 slices (the default) the red arrow and the grey dot almost coincide for every turn. Slide the slices down to 4 and they separate. Slide up to 100,000 and they merge.
  • Press π/2. The machine lands on i, straight up. A quarter of the string is a quarter-turn. That is what i was in Section 4. So eiπ/2 = i.
  • Press 2π. Back to 1, a full lap. More on that in Stranger things.

Roger Cotes wrote down the link between "string length" and the machine in 1714. His form is hard to recognise today, because he wrote it with logarithms. He died two years later, at 33. Newton said "if he had lived we might have known something". Leonhard Euler stated the general fact in his 1748 book Introductio in analysin infinitorum: the machine fed a turn lands on the circle. Euler never wrote the line eiπ + 1 = 0 as such. It is one special case of his formula. The habit of writing it that way, and calling it "Euler's identity", came in the 19th and 20th centuries. The physicist Richard Feynman, in his 1964 lectures, called the general formula "our jewel".

Interlude
Stranger things

  • A full lap: e2πi = 1. Feed the machine a turn of 2π. The arrow walks all the way round and lands back on 1. In Section 9's sim, press 2π. The equation looks like it should be exciting. It just says "a full turn gets you home".
  • i to the power i is an ordinary number: about 0.208. Here's why. i is where the machine lands when fed a turn of π/2 (press π/2 above). Raising to a power multiplies the thing you fed in. So i to the power i means feeding i × iπ/2. That is −π/2, because i × i = −1. A feed of −π/2 is a shrink, not a turn: the bank machine with growth −π/2. The result is e−π/2 = 0.2079. For older readers: it isn't the only value. You could also reach i by walking π/2 plus any number of full laps. 0.208 is the one everyone means by default.
  • Your house electricity is a spinning arrow. The voltage in a wall socket swings back and forth 50 times a second (60 in some countries). Engineers picture it as an arrow spinning round the number plane. They read off its up-and-down shadow. The multiplication-is-turning idea makes the sums easy. Charles Steinmetz pushed this method in 1893. It is still how electrical engineers work.
  • A pure musical note is a spinning arrow too. A tuning fork spins 440 times a second. Its shadow traces the wave you'd see on a sound app. Every sound you hear is a pile of spinning arrows, at different speeds, added together.
  • The insult that stuck. "Imaginary" (Descartes, 1637) was meant to be dismissive, like calling something "so-called". Gauss later said it would have been better to call them "lateral" numbers: sideways numbers. He was right, and it's too late.
  • Bernoulli's problem (1683) was about interest paid in ever-smaller slices. It is the first place e appears in history. He never knew its value.
  • Three people drew the plane. Wessel (1797), Argand (1806), Gauss (1831, though he had the idea by 1799). Wessel's paper was in Danish and was ignored for a century. The picture is sometimes still called an "Argand diagram".
  • The turn machine is in your phone. When a game rotates a character, the code multiplies by a number like the orange dot in Section 5. A GPS working out a heading does the same. One multiplication that stretches and turns is simply cheaper than doing the two separately.

Section 10
Make your own

You've seen the machine fed a straight slice. That's the bank, and it lands on e. You've seen it fed a sideways slice. That's the turn, and it lands on the circle. The capstone is to feed it both at once: a bit of growth and a bit of turn. Growth makes the arrow longer as it goes. Turn makes it curl. Together they make a spiral. Make your own, and predict where it lands before you look.

The full machine: keep the arrow, add a slice that is part straight and part sideways, repeat. Growth sets how fast the spiral grows or shrinks. Turn sets how far round it goes.

A few things to notice

  • Growth 0.5, turn 6.28: one full lap. The arrow ends close to 1.65 = e0.5. (The readout shows how close, and the exact answer.) The length only cares about growth. The direction only cares about turn.
  • Set growth to 0: a perfect circle again (Section 7). Set turn to 0: the bank again (Section 6). No spiral, just a longer arrow.
  • Negative growth shrinks. Try growth −1, turn 12.6: two laps inwards. That is the shape of a snail shell, a hurricane and a galaxy's arm.
  • Make a prediction game. The parent picks growth and turn. The child says where it lands: length egrowth, direction = turn. Then slide to check.
The whole mechanism in one sentence: a power is a tiny step repeated, a sideways step only turns the arrow, so tiny sideways steps walk it round a circle, and π of that walk is exactly halfway round, at −1.

Next time someone says "it links the five most important constants", you can say: "Walk halfway round a circle, step right once, and you're home. The five constants are just the names of the things in that sentence."

What the sims model, honestly. Every picture is computed live from the real arithmetic. Arrows are complex numbers. Multiplication is the actual complex product.
