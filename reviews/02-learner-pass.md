# Learner pass: the two Euler pages read as a 10-year-old

Method: each section read as a reasonably smart 10-year-old working alone, with no adult. For every section I logged (a) words used before they are explained, (b) steps where the child is told to accept something, (c) sim instructions a child could not carry out, (d) where attention runs out. Reading level was measured separately: every section of both pages is Flesch-Kincaid grade 4 to 5, sentences of 9 to 12 words. Sentence difficulty is not the issue anywhere. Concept load is.

## Verdict in one table

| | Let's Squash a Football | Spinning to Minus One |
|---|---|---|
| Alone, completes with understanding | 11 to 12 | 13 to 14 |
| With a parent beside them | 9 to 10 | 11 to 12 |
| A 10-year-old alone gets | sections 1 to 7 fully, the 12-pentagon idea loosely, section 10 skimmed | sections 1 to 3, the bank story, the string-round-a-plate idea, all four home activities, and the one-sentence gist |
| Sittings needed | 2 (break after section 7) | 2 to 3 (break after section 5, and after 7) |
| Sections a child cannot do alone | 8 (shares), 9 (shares again), the rules list in 10 | 4 (bracket algebra), 5 second half (the x and n machine), 6 "why it bends", 8 (θ and e^iθ) |

The polyhedra page is an honest 10-year-old page with two hard steps. The identity page is a good 12-to-14 page wearing a 10-year-old label.

## Let's Squash a Football

### What works for a child

- Opening: picking up the ball and counting 12 patches is something they can actually do. The five questions are all concrete.
- Sections 1 to 3: box, folds, sharp points; dropdown names carry helper labels ("Octahedron (a gemstone)"); the rubber-shape sim and the dough-and-straws box land the "what's joined to what" idea physically.
- Sections 5 and 6: fields and fences is a good picture. "Now run the film backwards" is the clearest statement of a proof by induction a child will meet.
- Section 11: "Check your shape" gives useful replies for 0, for non-2 totals, and for junk input.
- Ending: "A donut and a coffee mug are the same shape" is the right last image.

### Where a child gets stuck, in page order

1. **"Prove" is used before it is shown.** Intro: "you'll be able to prove it yourself." Section 2: "He couldn't prove it. By section 6, you will have a proof." A 10-year-old reads "prove" as "check." The page does eventually show what a proof is (section 6), but one line early would help: "checking 20 shapes isn't proving; proving means showing it can never fail."
2. **Section 2's table gives the answer away.** The "Corners + faces" column is on screen before the child looks. The discovery promised ("find the pattern for themselves") is reduced to comparing two given columns. Better: columns for corners, edges, faces only, then a "Try adding corners and faces" nudge in the notice list, then the reveal.
3. **Section 3 home box will not work as written.** Drinking straws pushed into soft roti dough fall out when the cube is leaned or pressed. A child who tries it gets a collapsed heap, not a "squashy cube." Plasticine or clay holds straws; dough does not. Swap the material or warn.
4. **Section 4: the key leap is accepted, not seen.** "Every bit of that outside used to be the popped face, stretched out enormously. So we count the outside as one face." The sim shows a Schlegel-style zoom, which looks like flying into the shape, not like stretching a face. A child accepts it because the readout says the counts match. Acceptable, but it is the one "trust me" in the first half. A single frame showing the popped face's rim growing to become the picture border would close it.
5. **Section 4 net box is heavier than it looks.** Counting 14 corners and 19 edges on a cross net means counting T-junctions as corners and then following "seven pairs of edges meet and become one each" while folding. Doable, but it is the longest counting job on the page and arrives after a long section. Move it to section 11 as a second make-your-own, or trim it to "count the squares: 6, plus the outside."
6. **Section 5: "taking away a take-away makes it bigger."** "Edges are the part we take away, so losing an edge makes the total 1 bigger." This is the one piece of arithmetic that trips 10-year-olds, and the text gives no numbers. Add the worked line: "8 − 12 + 6 = 2. Snip one: 8 − 11 + 5 = 2."
7. **Section 7's sim has three gestures and no cue.** "Tap the middle of a line, tap two dots, or tap an empty spot" is the caption. A child taps once, sees nothing obvious, and moves on. "Add one for me" rescues it, but a first tap should produce a visible hint ("now tap a second dot").
8. **Section 8 is the hard step, and it is as gentle as it can be.** "Fair share" with 6 ÷ 3 and 6 ÷ 2 is division as sharing, applied twice, then grouped six pentagons to avoid fractions. A smart 10-year-old gets it on a second read. Two things make it harder than it needs to be: the section opens with "Here's the trick," which signals cleverness and raises anxiety; and the sentence "a hexagon adds nothing to the total!" comes before the child has seen why a share is a sensible thing to add up. A one-line bridge ("if every patch adds up its own share, the shares add up to the whole ball") would help.
9. **Section 8: "Try 12 and 1 … nobody can actually build that ball"** is asserted with no reason. The page has just taught the child that passing the count is the rule. A smart child asks "why not?" and gets nothing. Either cut it or say "that's a different puzzle, and no one has a one-line answer."
10. **Section 9: "A dome is a football turned inside out"** reads like an explanation and is not one (it means the dual). A child will try to picture turning a ball inside out and fail. Say "every red corner sits where a pentagon would be."
11. **Section 10 is the longest section (563 words) and the most abstract.** The four-rule list ("every edge touches exactly two faces, and the surface never pinches to a single point"; "faces don't pass through each other") is definition language. "Count it as 12 big stars" with faces passing through each other is a graduate idea in a checkbox. A child will skim the whole section, which is fine for a "Stranger" section, but the stacked-cube example (16 − 24 + 11 = 3) is not in the sim, so the child cannot check it and must take the 24 on trust. Put it in the sim or drop the numbers.
12. **Length.** About 4,400 words plus 11 sims plus 4 home boxes. A 10-year-old does sections 1 to 7 in one sitting of 40 to 50 minutes and should stop there, because section 7 ends the proof. A visible "good place to stop" marker after section 7 would help.

### Things a child is never told

- Why the tree of sticks always has a loose end (asserted in section 6). Fine to assert, but say "try to draw a tree with no tips."
- What "random" means in "Lumpy pushes each corner in or out by a random amount." Minor.

## Spinning to Minus One

### What works for a child

- Sections 1 to 3 are excellent at age 10: arrows that stretch, ×(−1) as a swing, "what could you do twice to make a half-turn?" The Brahmagupta debt line and the "imaginary was an insult" line are the kind of history a child repeats at dinner.
- The bank story in section 5 is concrete, uses rupees, and the calculator race is a genuinely good home activity.
- Section 7's string round a roti is the best hands-on item on either page.
- The ball-on-a-string picture in section 6 is the right physical analogy for "sideways pushes make a circle."
- The page is honest: every place it cannot prove something, it says so and labels the box "For older readers."

### Where a child gets stuck, in page order

1. **"Raised to the power" is assumed from the first paragraph.** The intro asks "How do you even multiply something by itself an imaginary number of times?" That sentence only lands if the child already reads e^x as repeated multiplication. Many 10-year-olds know "squared" and nothing more. Section 1 has "2 × 2 × 2 = 8" and could add "which people write 2³, a little 3 meaning three times." Without it, the superscripts on the T-shirt are decoration all the way to section 8.
2. **Section 3's notice list uses notation section 4 introduces.** "Drag the tip to 2 + 1i (two right, one up) and press ×i. It swings to −1 + 2i." The form "2 + 1i" is defined in the first paragraph of section 4. Swap the order or write "two right, one up" only.
3. **Section 4 is the wall.** "(1 + i)(1 + i) = 1 + i + i + i×i = 1 + 2i − 1 = 2i" with the words "You use ordinary multiplication." For a 10-year-old this is not ordinary multiplication. Expanding two brackets is taught at 13 to 14. Everything after depends on "angles add, lengths multiply," which the page then verifies only by dragging handles and admits ("checking isn't proving"). For the stated audience the honest version is: drop the expansion into the "For older readers" box, keep the sim, and state the rule as the thing to check with the handles. The section would then be a 10-year-old section.
4. **Section 5 second half: the "machine."** The bank story is fine. Then in five lines the child meets n ("where n is how many payments"), the notation (1 + 1/n)^n, then x ("You feed it a number x. It splits x into n tiny pieces"), then e^x "for any x." Three new letters and a power with a letter in it. The two concrete lines that follow ("Feed it x = 1 and out comes e. Feed it x = 2 … about 7.39") are the ones a child can hold. Lead with those and keep the letters in a side note: "the grown-up way to write the machine is (1 + x/n)^n."
5. **Section 6, "Why does it bend into a circle?"** The argument is distributivity: multiplying by (1 + iπ/n) keeps the arrow and adds a quarter-turned slice of it. A child can get this through the bank: "the bank keeps your money and adds a slice; this machine keeps your arrow and adds a sideways slice." The page does not make that link. It is one sentence and it would carry the whole section.
6. **Section 6 readouts show noise.** "stretches it 1.0000197×" and "turned 359.9953°." To a child seven decimals are not "nearly 1," they are a strange number. Round to "1.00002×" and "360.0°" with a note that the readout is rounded.
7. **Section 7's link back uses letters where numbers would do.** "Each step was a sideways push of π/n times an arrow roughly 1 long. Add up n of those pushes and you've walked about π." The 8-step case is right there: "8 pushes of 0.39 make 3.14." Say that first.
8. **Section 8 is pure notation.** θ ("the Greek letter theta") is the third new letter. "In section 5 we called the machine's answer e^x. So when we feed it x = iθ, the answer is e^iθ." The climax of the page is a substitution. A child who skipped the letters in section 5 is lost here, and this is the section that delivers the T-shirt. One fix: keep the words and demote the symbols. "e to the something means: run the machine with that something. Run it with 'a turn of π' and you walk halfway round, to −1." Then show the symbols once.
9. **Section 9, i to the power i.** "Raise both sides to the power i. The power rules say you multiply the powers." No power rule has been taught. This item belongs entirely in a "For older readers" box; at present only the second half of it is.
10. **Section 10's notice bullets contradict the readout** at default settings (covered in the main review: "lands near 1.22" while the readout shows 1.49). A child notices this kind of thing and loses trust in the page.
11. **The ruler home box is fiddly.** Eight steps, each "0.39 times as long as" the previous arrow, each at a right angle. It needs a set square, a calculator and 15 minutes of care, and the error compounds. It is a good activity for a patient 12-year-old. For a 10-year-old, four steps with a pre-printed template would do.
12. **Length and sittings.** About 4,000 words and 8 sims. Natural stops: after section 3 (the child now owns i), after section 5 (the child owns e), then 6 to 8 in one go with help.

### Things a child is never told

- What "°" means or that a full turn is 360°. Used from section 3. Most 10-year-olds know it; a clause would cost nothing.
- Why the dashed circle in section 4 has "radius 1" and what radius means. Defined only in section 7.
- "Anticlockwise" (sections 3 and 7). Fine for most.

## What this means for the prompts

- The polyhedra prompt's "10-year-olds" is honest if the page carries fixes 2, 6, 8 and 9 above. The prompt should ask for a worked numeric line at every "the total stays the same" moment, and for the table to withhold the hint column.
- The identity prompt should say "a strong 12-to-14-year-old, or a 10-year-old reading with a parent," and should ask for three things it does not: the bracket expansion goes in an older-readers box; every letter (n, x, θ) is introduced after the concrete number it stands for; and the climax is written in words first, symbols second.
- Both prompts should ask for a marked "good place to stop" halfway, since neither page is a single sitting at this age.
